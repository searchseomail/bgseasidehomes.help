How to Spot Bankrupt or Problematic Developers in Bulgaria Before Paying a Deposit

PixelPioneer OP
05.04.2025 13:59
I am currently looking to buy an off-plan apartment in Sofia and found a great deal, but I am terrified of running into a bankrupt developer and losing my hard-earned savings. What are the red flags I should look out for during the background check, and which public registers in Bulgaria can I use to verify their financial health? Are there any specific clauses I should insist on adding to the preliminary contract to protect my deposit?

Buying off-plan in Sofia is definitely nerve-wracking, but you are right to be cautious. First thing you should check is the Bulgarian Commercial Register (Tarnovregisters) to see the developer's annual financial statements. If they are showing massive debts, zero revenue, or constant lawsuits, run. Also, never pay a deposit into a personal account; it must go to a special escrow or company account.

You need to check the 'CADA' (Registry Agency) for any mortgages or encumbrances on the land plot. If the land is mortgaged to a bank by the developer, make sure the preliminary contract specifies that the bank will release your specific apartment from the mortgage once it's built.

A huge red flag is if the price is significantly lower than the current market average in Sofia. If it sounds too good to be true, it usually means they are desperate for cash flow to finish older, stalled projects. Before committing to Sofia, some buyers also look at coastal investments, and you can read more about luxury gated communities in Sveti Vlas to understand how reputable developers usually structure their projects.

Always hire an independent lawyer who doesn't work with the agency or the developer. They can dig into the builder's history on the Chamber of Builders in Bulgaria (KSB) register. Make sure the developer has a valid license and no active insolvency proceedings.

Regarding contract clauses, insist on a strict penalty clause (neustoyka) for delayed Act 16 issuance. Furthermore, the preliminary contract should state that your deposit is fully refundable with interest if the building permit gets revoked or construction hasn't reached certain milestones by a hard deadline.

Check the Central Register of Debtors as well. If the owner or the construction company has pending enforcement cases with private bailiffs (chastni sadebni izpelniteli), that is an immediate dealbreaker. They are likely insolvent.

Never sign anything without a clause stating that installments are tied strictly to construction milestones (e.g., Act 14, Act 15, Act 16) rather than arbitrary calendar dates. If you pay upfront without proof of progress, you lose all your leverage if they go bankrupt.

If the developer refuses to include a clause for a bank guarantee or refuses to sign the preliminary contract before a notary (even though it's not strictly mandatory for a preliminary contract, it provides extra security), walk away immediately. Protect your hard-earned cash at all costs.
